There are periods in life when people seem to become different versions of themselves.

A reserved engineer suddenly finds himself talking for hours with strangers in a hostel kitchen. A widow joins a community choir and forms friendships that would have seemed impossible a year earlier. Neighbors who barely exchanged greetings spend entire days helping one another after a flood. A newcomer in a foreign country accepts invitations she would have politely declined back home. Conversations deepen. Boundaries soften. The social world becomes more permeable.

Most of us have experienced such periods. We may have encountered them while traveling, after a personal crisis, during military service, at university, after moving to a new city, or in the aftermath of a collective disaster. Looking back, these episodes often appear almost dreamlike. For a few weeks, months, or years, life seems charged with an unusual social electricity. People become curious about one another. New friendships form rapidly. The familiar calculations of status, profession, age, wealth, and background become less important. Human beings appear more available.

Social scientists have long noticed this phenomenon. Although no single theory fully explains it, several frameworks together provide a compelling map of this territory: Victor Turner's concepts of liminality and communitas, Émile Durkheim's theory of collective effervescence, and modern research into the social dynamics of disasters. Taken together, they suggest that hyper-sociality is not a random occurrence. It emerges when the ordinary structures of life are temporarily suspended and people find themselves crossing a threshold between one world and another.

The anthropologist Victor Turner devoted much of his work to understanding what happens during periods of transition. Building on earlier studies of rites of passage, Turner observed that many cultures separate major life changes into three phases: separation, transition, and reintegration. The middle phase fascinated him most. He called it "liminality," from the Latin word limen, meaning threshold. During this period people are neither what they were nor yet what they will become. They exist in an in-between state, "betwixt and between" the normal categories that organize social life.

Liminality is often uncomfortable. The old map no longer works, and the new one has not yet appeared. Yet Turner believed that this uncertainty also creates unusual possibilities. When people leave behind familiar roles, routines, and expectations, they become more open to transformation. Social distinctions weaken. The normal architecture of identity becomes less rigid. In this threshold state, people often encounter one another more directly than they do under ordinary circumstances.

Consider what happens when someone moves to another country.

Migration is usually discussed in economic, legal, or demographic terms, but it is also one of the most profound liminal experiences available to an adult. The migrant leaves behind an entire social ecosystem. Friendships, habits, reputation, professional identity, and cultural assumptions lose some of their force. Even simple activities such as shopping, ordering food, or greeting neighbors require conscious attention.

At first glance this can feel like a loss. Yet it also creates freedom.

Back home, social life is often governed by invisible rails laid down over decades. We know who belongs to our circles and who does not. We know which invitations are natural and which would seem strange. We know where we fit.

Migration removes many of those rails.

The newcomer occupies a peculiar social position. Nobody knows their history. They can attend events outside their usual demographic group. They can befriend people twenty years older or younger than themselves. They can join activities that would have felt incompatible with their previous identity. The social field becomes fluid.

This helps explain why many expatriates and immigrants report becoming unusually social during their first years abroad. It is not simply that they need companionship, although that is certainly part of the story. It is also that they inhabit a liminal zone. They are temporarily released from the dense network of assumptions that structured their previous lives.

The same phenomenon appears among pilgrims, long-distance hikers, gap-year travelers, and participants in residential retreats. People who would normally pass one another without a second glance find themselves sharing stories, meals, fears, and aspirations. The temporary nature of the experience often intensifies the connection. Everyone knows the moment will not last. Paradoxically, this awareness can encourage openness.

Turner used another term for the special social atmosphere that emerges in such situations: communitas.

Communitas is not merely community. Communities can be hierarchical, bureaucratic, and exclusionary. Communitas refers to a more direct and egalitarian form of human connection that arises when social structure temporarily loosens its grip. During communitas, individuals encounter one another less as roles and more as fellow participants in a shared experience. Status differences become less important. A doctor, a laborer, a student, and a retiree may find themselves speaking as equals.

Most people recognize this feeling immediately once it is described.

It appears around campfires and pilgrimage routes. It emerges in volunteer projects, artistic collaborations, and temporary villages. It is present in many music festivals and communal gatherings. Participants often struggle to explain why these environments feel different from ordinary life. The explanation may be that they are experiencing communitas: a brief suspension of the social machinery that usually organizes interaction.

Durkheim approached a similar phenomenon from a different angle. He observed that when groups gather around a shared activity, they can enter a state he called collective effervescence. During these moments, emotional energy becomes synchronized across the group. People feel elevated, connected, and part of something larger than themselves. What might seem impossible alone becomes natural in the presence of others.

Collective effervescence is visible at concerts, sporting events, religious ceremonies, demonstrations, and celebrations. A crowd sings together. Thousands of people focus on a single performance. A shared emotional rhythm emerges. Participants often describe feeling more alive, more connected, and more open than usual. The effect can persist long after the event itself has ended.

Importantly, collective effervescence does not require agreement on every issue or even deep familiarity. What matters is synchronized attention and shared emotional engagement. For a time, individual concerns recede into the background. The group becomes psychologically real.

This brings us to one of the most surprising findings in social science: some of the strongest forms of communitas emerge not during celebrations but during disasters.

Popular imagination predicts chaos.

When people think about earthquakes, floods, hurricanes, blackouts, or wars, they often imagine panic, selfishness, and social breakdown. Films and novels reinforce this expectation. Yet decades of disaster research tell a different story.

Again and again, researchers have found that ordinary people respond to crises with extraordinary cooperation. Strangers help one another. Informal support networks emerge rapidly. Resources are shared. Social divisions that previously seemed important lose significance. The aftermath of catastrophe often produces a remarkable surge of solidarity. Researchers have described this phenomenon as disaster communitas.

The reason is not difficult to understand.

Disasters create a shared reality that cannot be ignored. Everyone faces the same threat. Existing institutions are frequently overwhelmed. Formal structures become less effective. Under such conditions, people turn toward one another.

The result is often a temporary society built from improvisation.

Neighbors who never spoke begin coordinating meals. Volunteers arrive without being asked. Information spreads through informal channels. The normal barriers between strangers become surprisingly porous. Researchers studying floods, earthquakes, and other disasters have repeatedly documented not only practical cooperation but also powerful feelings of meaning and connection. Survivors often describe these periods as some of the most socially intense experiences of their lives.

This sometimes produces what might be called a post-disaster high.

The phrase sounds insensitive until one understands what it means. Nobody enjoys the destruction, loss, or suffering. Yet many people report that the period immediately following a disaster contains a quality of human connection rarely encountered elsewhere. Life becomes urgent. Priorities become clear. People spend less energy on status and more on helping. Relationships feel more authentic. The world, despite its pain, appears strangely alive.

Writers who have documented disaster recovery often note a bittersweet pattern. The initial wave of solidarity eventually fades. Institutions recover. Bureaucracies return. Daily routines reassert themselves. The temporary society dissolves. Many survivors describe missing the sense of purpose and togetherness that emerged during the crisis.

This observation reveals something important about human nature.

The hyper-social periods we remember most vividly may not arise because life becomes easier. In many cases, the opposite is true. They emerge when familiar structures weaken and people must rely more directly on one another. Shared uncertainty, rather than shared comfort, often acts as the catalyst.

Seen through this lens, migration, disaster, pilgrimage, and communal celebration belong to the same family of experiences.

Each removes people from ordinary routines.

Each creates uncertainty.

Each weakens established social boundaries.

Each encourages encounters that would otherwise never occur.

And each generates conditions under which communitas and collective effervescence can flourish.

This does not mean that liminality is always pleasant. Threshold states can be lonely, frightening, and disorienting. Many migrants struggle with isolation. Disaster survivors endure profound suffering. Pilgrims experience exhaustion. Major life transitions often involve grief as well as possibility. Yet these same conditions can create opportunities for connection that structured life rarely permits.

Modern societies tend to prize stability, predictability, and efficiency. These are valuable achievements. But they may also reduce the frequency of experiences that generate communitas. The more tightly life is organized, the less often we encounter strangers outside our social category. The more specialized our roles become, the harder it is to meet one another simply as fellow human beings.

Perhaps this is why people continue to seek liminal experiences voluntarily. They travel. They attend festivals. They join retreats. They walk long trails. They volunteer after disasters. They move abroad. Consciously or not, they are entering spaces where the ordinary rules loosen and new forms of relationship become possible.

The longing beneath these choices may be surprisingly simple.

Human beings do not merely want security. We also want participation. We want to feel part of a living world populated by fellow travelers rather than isolated individuals occupying adjacent compartments. Liminal periods temporarily satisfy that longing. They create conditions under which people rediscover one another.

Perhaps the deeper lesson of liminality is that much of what we call personality is shaped by structure. We think some people are naturally sociable and others reserved, yet when familiar roles and boundaries dissolve, people often reveal a greater capacity for connection.

The migrant in a new country, the volunteer after a disaster, the pilgrim on a long road, and the stranger around a campfire all experience a temporary loosening of the structures that normally separate us. What emerges is more than friendliness: a heightened sense of shared presence and mutual dependence.

The post-disaster high and the intense sociability of migration may therefore reveal something fundamental about human nature: beneath the roles and routines of ordinary life lies a readiness for connection that becomes visible when the structures containing it loosen.
